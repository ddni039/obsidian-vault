# Edward Tufte — Wikipedia Summary

## Overview

American statistician, professor emeritus, writer, and sculptor (born March 14, 1942). Pioneer in data visualization and information design. Known as "The Leonardo da Vinci of data" (NYT) and "The Galileo of graphics" (Bloomberg).

## Education

- **BS & MS**: Statistics, Stanford University
- **PhD**: Political Science, Yale University (1968; dissertation: *The Civil Rights Movement and Its Opposition*)

## Key Concepts

### Chartjunk
Useless, non-informative, or information-obscuring elements in quantitative displays — decorative elements that do not aid the viewer.

### Lie Factor
The ratio of the size of an effect in a graphic to the size of the effect in the data. A well-designed graphic has Lie Factor close to 1.

### Data-Ink Ratio
The proportion of ink used for actual data versus decoration. Maximize data-ink ratio by removing non-data ink.

> "Sometimes decoration can help editorialize about the substance of the graphic. But it is wrong to distort the data measures—the ink locating values of numbers—in order to make an editorial comment or fit a decorative scheme."
> — Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, p. 59

### Data Density
The amount of data in a graphic relative to its area. High data density graphics use the available space efficiently.

### Design Techniques

#### Small Multiple
Charts showing multiple series on single axes, or several separate pairs of axes placed side-by-side. Especially useful when series use different y-axis scales but share the same x-axis range (typically time).

#### Sparklines
Condensed trend/variation graphics embedded directly in text — a visual "word" integrating seamlessly with text.

#### Macro/Micro Readings
Enable patterns to be grasped at a glance (macro) while details remain available on inspection (micro).

## Design Philosophy

- Use **data-rich illustrations** presenting all available data
- Enable **macro/micro readings** — patterns at a glance, details on inspection
- Present information within an **eye-span** at the **high resolution of printed page**
- Encourage viewing data at maximum **data density**
- **To clarify, add detail** — complexity is not noise, noise is noise
- **To simplify, differentiate** — don't remove complexity, change its character
- **Layering and separation** — separate out different types of information visually
- **Micro/macro readings** — design for both overview and detail simultaneously

## PowerPoint Criticism

*The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* (2003, also published in Wired):

Tufte criticized PowerPoint for:
- Guiding presenters rather than enlightening audiences
- Oversimplified tables and charts due to legacy low-resolution design
- Artificial hierarchy from outline structure
- Lockstep linear progression — audiences cannot browse at leisure
- Poor typography and chart layout
- Destroying narrative structure by squashing ideas into bullet points

Tufte cited NASA engineers' PowerPoint use as a prime example of its failures. His analysis of a NASA slide (an engineering detail buried in small type with six bullet points) appeared in the **Columbia Accident Investigation Board report**.

**Alternative**: For technical settings, distribute a brief written report (readable in 5–10 minutes) before devoting the meeting to discussion.

## Historical Exemplars

Tufte uses these as models of excellent data visualization:

- **Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 Carte Figurative** — Napoleon's invasion of Russia, showing 6 variables in 2 dimensions
- **John Snow's cholera map** — geographic data revealing the source of an epidemic
